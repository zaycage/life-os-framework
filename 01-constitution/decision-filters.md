# Decision Filters

> Good decisions compound. So do bad ones.

---

# What

Decision filters are a small set of questions applied before making meaningful decisions.

They exist to make good judgment repeatable.

A filter does not make the decision for you.

It reveals whether the decision aligns with who you are trying to become.

---

# Why

Most poor decisions are not caused by bad values.

They are caused by deciding under fatigue, emotion, or social pressure — without consulting your values at all.

A written filter moves judgment from memory to system.

You stop relying on being wise in the moment.

You rely on questions you wrote when you were thinking clearly.

---

# The Core Filter

Before an important decision, ask:

- Does this make me more capable?
- Does this strengthen what matters most to me?
- Does this align with my principles?
- Does this reduce recurring friction?
- Can I sustain this?

If the answers are mostly yes, the decision is probably aligned.

---

# The Tiebreaker

Filters can pass two conflicting options at once.

More opportunity and more presence can both answer yes.

When options conflict, ask the final question:

> Which choice will my ninety-year-old self be proud of?

The long view breaks most ties.

Short-term costs shrink over decades.

Regret does not.

---

# Principles

1. Decide by filter, not by mood.

2. The filter is consulted before the decision, not used to justify it afterward.

3. A filter you will not actually use is worse than no filter.

4. Few questions, consistently applied, beat many questions occasionally applied.

5. When two good options conflict, extend the time horizon.

---

# Applications

## Major Decisions

Career changes, large purchases, relocations, commitments of years.

Run the full filter.

Write the answers down.

---

## Recurring Decisions

Purchases, obligations, new projects.

Use the single most relevant question as a fast check.

For possessions: does this reduce recurring friction?

For commitments: can I sustain this?

---

## Reviews

Filters improve through use.

When a decision turns out poorly, ask which question would have caught it.

Add or sharpen one question.

Keep the filter short.
